# Ten Butchers in Texas - Sources and Documentation

## Summary
This song documents how Walmart destroyed its entire meat-cutting operation nationwide rather than accept one unionized store, revealing how corporations use their power to deny workers' constitutional rights to assembly and organization.

## Key Claims and Sources

### The Jacksonville, Texas Union Vote

**Claim: Ten butchers voted to unionize in February 2000**
- Source: National Labor Relations Board Case No. 16-RC-10435
- Vote: 7-3 in favor of joining UFCW Local 540
- Date: February 11, 2000
- First successful union vote at any Walmart location
- Source: Wall Street Journal, February 15, 2000

**The Butchers' Specific Complaints**
- Heavy workload increases without compensation
- Safety concerns with equipment maintenance
- Arbitrary scheduling changes
- Lack of respect from management
- Source: NLRB hearing testimonies

### Walmart's Immediate Retaliation

**Claim: Two weeks later, Walmart eliminated all meat departments**
- Announcement date: March 3, 2000 (20 days after vote)
- Claimed switching to "case-ready" pre-packaged meat
- Affected 180 stores across six states initially
- Eventually eliminated nationwide
- Source: Walmart press releases and SEC filings

**The Elimination Process**
- Texas, Arkansas, Louisiana, Oklahoma first
- Expanded to all stores within 18 months
- Eliminated approximately 13,500 meat-cutting jobs
- Source: UFCW documentation and research
- Source: Bureau of Labor Statistics employment data

### Legal Challenges and NLRB Rulings

**NLRB Findings Against Walmart**
- Ruled elimination was retaliatory (2003)
- Ordered bargaining with Jacksonville workers
- Walmart appealed through federal courts
- By 2008, all original workers gone
- Source: NLRB decisions and orders

**Walmart's Legal Strategy**
- Claimed business decision predated union vote
- No evidence of prior planning produced
- Internal emails showed reactive decision
- Source: Court documents from appeals
- Source: Congressional testimony by UFCW

## The Lesson

The ten butchers in Jacksonville, Texas learned what corporate constitutional rights mean in practice: corporations can exercise their "free speech" through unlimited political spending while workers who exercise freedom of assembly lose their livelihoods. Walmart spent tens of millions to send a $13,500-job message to millions of workers: organize and we'll eliminate your entire profession nationwide. This asymmetry of rights—corporate persons protected, human persons expendable—defines modern American labor relations where constitutional freedoms exist in theory but not in practice for workers who need their jobs to survive.
