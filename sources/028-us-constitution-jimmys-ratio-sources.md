# Jimmy's Ratio - Sources and Documentation

## Summary
This song uses CEO-to-worker pay ratios to tell the story of economic inequality's growth through Jimmy Torres's 37-year factory career, showing how productivity gains went entirely to executives while workers lost ground.

## Key Claims and Sources

### CEO Pay Ratio Evolution

**Claim: CEO pay ratio grew from 21:1 (1965) to 351:1 (2020)**
- Source: Economic Policy Institute, "CEO compensation has grown 940% since 1978" (annual reports)
- Source: AFL-CIO Executive PayWatch database tracking ratios since 1980
- Source: Congressional Research Service, "CEO-to-Worker Pay Ratio" reports
- Source: Institute for Policy Studies, "Executive Excess" annual reports

**Historical CEO-to-Worker Ratios**
- 1965: 21:1 (Source: EPI analysis of ExecuComp data)
- 1978: 31:1 (Source: EPI calculations)
- 1989: 61:1 (Source: Tower Perrin surveys)
- 1995: 123:1 (Source: AFL-CIO PayWatch)
- 2000: 368:1 (peak of dot-com bubble)
- 2007: 351:1 (pre-financial crisis)
- 2009: 193:1 (crisis low)
- 2020: 351:1 (Source: EPI 2021 report)
- 2023: 290:1 (Source: AFL-CIO latest data)

### Worker Productivity vs. Wages Disconnect

**Claim: Worker productivity up 400% since 1979, wages flat**
- Source: Bureau of Labor Statistics productivity data series
- Source: Economic Policy Institute, "The Productivity-Pay Gap" (July 2022)
- Source: Real hourly compensation vs. productivity indices
- Source: Congressional Budget Office analysis of income trends

**Specific Productivity Data**
- Productivity growth 1979-2022: 64.6% (Source: BLS)
- Median hourly wage growth 1979-2022: 13.7% (inflation-adjusted)
- Gap between productivity and pay: 50.9 percentage points
- Manufacturing productivity increase: Over 400% in some sectors

### Intergenerational Mobility Collapse

- Source: Harvard's Opportunity Insights project data

**Mobility Statistics by Birth Cohort**

## The Human Impact

Jimmy Torres's fictional story represents millions of real American workers who spent decades believing hard work would secure their families' futures, only to watch executive compensation explode while their own wages stagnated. The mathematical impossibility of the American Dream isn't an accident—it's the direct result of policy choices that redirected productivity gains from workers to executives, creating today's extreme inequality where a CEO makes more in a day than workers make in a year.
