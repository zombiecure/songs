# Susette's Pink House - Sources and Documentation

## Summary
This song tells how Kelo v. New London (2005) perverted property rights by allowing governments to seize private homes for corporate development, following Susette Kelo's fight to keep her lovingly restored pink house.

## Key Claims and Sources

### The Kelo Case

**Claim: Susette Kelo's pink house was taken for corporate development**
- Source: Kelo v. City of New London, 545 U.S. 469 (2005)
- Source: Jeff Benedict, "Little Pink House" (2009) - comprehensive case history
- Source: Institute for Justice case files and documentation
- Source: New London Day newspaper archives 2000-2005

**Susette Kelo's Story**
- Purchased house November 1997 for $53,500
- Spent years renovating with her own hands
- House built in 1893, restored to Victorian colors
- Located at 8 East Street, Fort Trumbull neighborhood
- Source: Property records, New London County

**Claim: Pfizer's development never materialized, land sits empty**
- Source: New York Times, "Pfizer to Leave New London" (Nov 2009)
- Source: Wall Street Journal coverage of abandoned development
- Source: Current Google Earth images showing vacant lots
- Source: New London economic development reports post-Kelo

### Supreme Court Decision Impact

**Claim: 5-4 decision expanded eminent domain for private benefit**
- Majority: Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer
- Dissent: O'Connor, Rehnquist, Scalia, Thomas
- Source: Official Supreme Court opinion and dissents
- Source: Oral argument transcripts showing justice concerns

**Justice O'Connor's Dissent Quote**
- "Any property may now be taken for the benefit of another private party"
- "The beneficiaries are likely to be those citizens with disproportionate influence"
- Source: O'Connor dissent in Kelo, pages 494-505
- Called decision a "reverse Robin Hood" outcome

## The Bitter Irony

Susette Kelo's pink house was destroyed for a development that never came, leaving empty lots where a neighborhood once stood. The Supreme Court's decision allowing private property transfer for speculative economic benefit created a precedent that threatens every American's property rights, proving Justice O'Connor's warning that "the specter of condemnation hangs over all property." The land where Susette's pink house stood remains vacant twenty years later—a monument to the Court's catastrophic error in prioritizing corporate development over individual rights.
