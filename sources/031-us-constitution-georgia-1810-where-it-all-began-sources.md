# Georgia, 1810 - Sources and Documentation

## Summary
This song reveals the origin of corporate constitutional rights in Fletcher v. Peck (1810), where the Supreme Court protected a corrupt land deal, establishing the precedent that corporate interests override democratic correction.

## Key Claims and Sources

### The Yazoo Land Fraud

**Claim: 35 million acres corruptly sold through legislative bribery**
- Source: C. Peter Magrath, "Yazoo: Law and Politics in the New Republic" (1966)
- Date: January 7, 1795 - Georgia legislature sold 35 million acres for $500,000
- Area: Most of present-day Alabama and Mississippi
- Price: 1.5 cents per acre when land normally sold for $1-2
- Source: Georgia legislative records and contemporary accounts

**The Corruption Details**
- Every legislator but one had shares in purchasing companies
- Bribes documented in contemporary newspapers
- Senator James Jackson exposed the corruption
- Source: Augusta Chronicle archives (1795-1796)
- Source: Charles Sellers, "The Market Revolution" (1991)

**The Four Purchasing Companies**
1. Georgia Company
2. Georgia-Mississippi Company
3. Upper Mississippi Company
4. Tennessee Company
- Source: Original legislative acts and company records

### The Democratic Response

### Fletcher v. Peck Decision

**Claim: Marshall ruled contracts sacred even when fraudulent**
- Opinion delivered March 16, 1810
- 4-1 decision (Justice Cushing absent)
- First time Court overturned state law
- Source: Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. 87 (1810)
- Source: Marshall's complete opinion text

**Marshall's Key Holdings**
- Contracts Clause prevents legislative rescission
- Courts cannot inquire into legislative motives
- Third-party purchasers protected despite fraud
- "A contract is a compact between two or more parties"
- Source: Direct quotes from opinion

**Justice Johnson's Dissent**
- Argued natural law against enforcing fraud
- Corruption voids contracts from beginning
- Legislature has power to correct mistakes
- Source: Johnson's dissenting opinion

## The Poisoned Seed

Fletcher v. Peck planted what the song calls "America's poisoned seed"—the principle that corporate contracts, even those born of corruption, override democratic governance. From this 1810 decision protecting land fraud grew the entire tree of corporate constitutional rights that today allows corporations to dominate American politics and economics. The Georgia farmers and Creek and Cherokee peoples who lost their lands to protected corruption were the first victims of a system that continues to prioritize corporate power over human needs, establishing that in America, paper contracts matter more than people's democratic will.
